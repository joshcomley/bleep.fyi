Is it true that a record 1.3 million migrants are on Universal Credit?

Almost 1.3 million people without British or Irish citizenship or a right of abode were on Universal Credit in May 2026 — a real figure from the government's own official statistics, published 18 August 2026. But the share of Universal Credit claimants this group represents has been falling for two years, from 17.5% in June 2024 to 15.5% in May 2026, and Reform UK itself corrected a claim that the share was "rising" after a fact-checker queried the wording. Most of the record 8.4 million total is mechanical, not migration-driven: a government programme is transferring people off older legacy benefits like tax credits onto Universal Credit, which alone explains most of the recent increase. Most of the 1.3 million are people the rules already require to be settled here: well over half hold EU Settlement Scheme status dating from around Brexit, and most of the rest have indefinite leave to remain or refugee status, both generally requiring a residence or protection test to be granted in the first place. People with EU Settlement Scheme status are also the group most likely to be in paid work while on Universal Credit, at 57.4%, well above the 35.6% rate for British claimants — Universal Credit is substantially an in-work benefit topping up low wages, not only support for people who are unemployed. Asylum seekers and people with no lawful immigration status cannot claim Universal Credit at all; asylum seekers instead receive separate Home Office support worth £49.18 a week. Can asylum seekers or people in the UK illegally claim Universal Credit?

No. Universal Credit is legally classed as a "public fund", and anyone subject to a No Recourse to Public Funds condition — which applies to most people on a temporary work, study or family visa, and to anyone with no lawful immigration status — cannot claim it. Asylum seekers specifically cannot claim mainstream benefits at all while their claim is being decided; instead, under a completely separate Home Office system, they can get housing plus a cash allowance currently worth £49.18 a week per person (or £9.95 a week if their accommodation already provides meals). People generally become eligible for Universal Credit once they are granted settlement (indefinite leave to remain), refugee status, or another form of protection — statuses that require meeting a residence or protection test first, not simply arriving in the country.

The answer above describes settled immigration law on who can claim Universal Credit, not a contested statistic, and those rules have not changed between April 2022 and May 2026, the period this page covers. It does not address enforcement — whether the rules are always correctly applied in practice — only what the rules themselves say.

What can be checked, and what cannot

This week's viral claims about migrants on Universal Credit rest on a real, official figure. Here is the line between what DWP's own data and the law actually show, and what remains genuinely unpublished or unresolved.

Who can actually claim

  • Universal Credit is legally a "public fund": anyone with a No Recourse to Public Funds condition on their immigration status — most people on a work, study or family visa — cannot claim it.

    Home Office, Public funds: caseworker guidance

    Sources:https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/public-funds

  • Settlement (indefinite leave to remain) is generally the point migrants become eligible for Universal Credit; refugees and other protected-status holders are generally eligible without the No Recourse to Public Funds restriction.

    Home Office, Public funds: caseworker guidance

    Sources:https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/public-funds

  • Asylum seekers cannot claim Universal Credit while their claim is decided. They instead receive Home Office accommodation plus a cash allowance of £49.18 a week per person (or £9.95 if meals are provided).

    Home Office, Asylum support: what you'll get

    Sources:https://www.gov.uk/asylum-support

  • People with no lawful immigration status in the UK have No Recourse to Public Funds too, the same restriction that applies to most temporary visa holders, and cannot claim.

    Home Office, Public funds: caseworker guidance

    Sources:https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/public-funds

What the data cannot show

  • There is no official breakdown of Universal Credit spending by immigration status; DWP has confirmed to a fact-checker that it does not publish one.

    Full Fact, 21 August 2026

    Sources:https://fullfact.org/immigration/what-proportion-of-universal-credit-goes-to-non-british-citizens/

  • There is no individual-nationality breakdown (for example, how many Polish or Nigerian nationals) — only the 8 broad immigration-status categories used throughout this page.

    DWP, Universal Credit immigration status and nationality statistics

    Sources:https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/universal-credit-quarterly-statistics-29-april-2013-to-14-may-2026

  • Naturalisation lag means the true current non-British/Irish Universal Credit claimant count is probably lower than the published 1.3 million, not higher, per DWP's own published caveat.

    DWP bulletin text, Table 1 note

    Sources:https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/universal-credit-quarterly-statistics-29-april-2013-to-14-may-2026

The claims vs the record

  • Reform UK's welfare plan said the migrant share of Universal Credit "rose to 15.5%" in January 2026; DWP's own data shows the share has been FALLING since June 2024, and Reform corrected its wording after a fact-checker queried it.

    Full Fact, 21 August 2026

    Sources:https://fullfact.org/immigration/what-proportion-of-universal-credit-goes-to-non-british-citizens/

  • "Nearly 1.5 million migrants on benefits" (May 2026) and "almost 1.3 million" (August 2026) are not competing figures: one counts everyone who claimed at any point across a full year, the other counts who was actively claiming on one date. Both trace to DWP data.

    The Express, 7 May 2026, citing a DWP freedom-of-information response

    Sources:https://www.express.co.uk/news/uk/2202653/migrants-universal-credit-claims-uk-food-bank-world-shock-figures

  • A widely-shared, AI-labelled social media post stated specific figures that are more misapplied than invented: its "899,000" EU Settlement Scheme figure and "more than £10 billion" 2024 spending figure both trace to real, differently-scoped disclosures (an annual-flow FOI count and a household-level expenditure FOI), applied to the wrong period and the wrong unit without saying so.

    X post by @britmatters, 19 August 2026, explicitly labelled "Made with AI"

    Sources:https://www.telegraph.co.uk/business/2026/08/19/record-number-of-foreigners-claiming-benefits/

Three specific things remain unpublished: Universal Credit spending broken down by immigration status, how long claimants in each status have lived in the UK, and any individual-nationality breakdown finer than the 8 broad categories DWP currently uses. Anyone quoting a precise cost figure for "migrants on Universal Credit" is using an estimate or a different, household-level FOI-derived measure, not an official DWP person-level statistic.

What this data can and cannot tell you

  • DWP's own data says the 1.3 million non-UK/Irish Universal Credit count is likely OVERSTATED, not understated: someone who becomes a British citizen without telling DWP continues to be recorded under their old, non-CTA status (DWP's category for anyone without British or Irish citizenship or a right of abode) until they notify the department.
  • The 1.3 million non-UK/Irish figure counts everyone without British/Irish citizenship or a right of abode who had a live Universal Credit claim in May 2026, including claims with no payment that month; a narrower measure — non-UK nationals actually receiving a payment — was 13%, around 1.1 million, in December 2025.
  • These are people, not households or children: a claimant's British-born or British-citizen children and partners are not separately counted inside this 1.3 million, and DWP's immigration-status figures say nothing about household composition.
  • 0.3% of claimants — about 23,000 people — have no immigration status recorded on DWP's systems at all, a data gap rather than a confirmed migrant count, and this page's 1.3 million total includes them because that is how DWP itself groups the figures.
  • A known data error affected two categories, not the total: between 18 December 2024 and 7 May 2025, DWP wrongly recorded up to 34,000 refugee-status claimants as Humanitarian Protection instead; both categories sit inside the same 1.3 million total, which is unaffected, and DWP corrected the recording from June 2025 but cannot fix the historical record.
  • The 15.5% and 15.2% figures both circulating this week for January 2026 are not a DWP revision between releases: they come from the same 18 August release, and the gap is a definitional choice about the 0.3% of claimants with no immigration status recorded, who count as non-CTA in the 15.5%-family figures (as Reform's claim did) but not in the 15.2% figure. DWP's own headline figures are genuinely revised release to release, as with any administrative series, but that is not what explains this specific gap.
  • There is no official Universal Credit spending figure broken down by immigration status; DWP has confirmed publicly that it does not publish one. A 2022-2024 estimate exists only for a related, household-level measure obtained under freedom of information law, which is a different measure from this page's person-count figures.
  • The population comparison on this page mixes two different definitions, both the source's own choice: non-UK/non-Irish NATIONALITY among Universal Credit payment recipients, against people BORN outside the UK and Ireland in the general population — related but not identical concepts.
