Is Ben-Gvir building gallows with viewing booths so people can watch Palestinian prisoners be hanged?

A viral post claims Ben-Gvir "is building gallows with viewing booths so people can watch Palestinian prisoners be hanged." The core of this is true, with two overshoots. Israel's Knesset passed a law on 30 March 2026, by 62 votes to 48 (of its 120 seats), requiring military courts to sentence defined categories of killing to death by hanging, unless the court finds special circumstances allowing life imprisonment. On 18 August 2026, National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir published video — from his own political party, with no independent inspection or confirmation — from a prison whose death-row wing is already built and whose hanging facility is now under construction, with no completion date given, saying in his own words that it will include viewing booths. First overshoot: his own wording limits viewing to bereaved victims' families holding a permit, not the general public. Second overshoot: the post presupposes prisoners will actually be hanged there, but no death sentence has yet been handed down under the law and no execution has taken place — the facility could be completed and never used. The law reaches Palestinians in practice through two separate mechanisms: its West Bank military courts, which try only Palestinians and foreign residents and explicitly exclude Israeli citizens by law, and a separate civilian-court offence that applies only to killing intended to deny the existence of the State of Israel — a motive test legal scholars say is unlikely to be attributed to a Jewish extremist. Multiple human-rights bodies, including a United Nations committee, call this discriminatory. A legal challenge against the law remains unresolved at Israel's High Court of Justice, which has declined to freeze it. Does Israel's new death-penalty law apply to Israeli Jews too?

No — Israel's new death-penalty law is written and structured so that it does not, in practice, apply to Israeli Jews. This is the law's own jurisdictional and definitional design, not yet a tested legal outcome: no prosecution has yet reached a death sentence under this law, so no court has actually applied it to anyone, Jewish or Palestinian. The law works through two separate tracks. In the West Bank's military courts — the only courts where this law's death sentence can apply, and where it applies as the sole available sentence unless the court finds special circumstances allowing life imprisonment — Israeli citizens and permanent residents are explicitly excluded by law from being tried at all; only Palestinians and foreign residents of the West Bank are tried there, while Israeli settlers who kill are tried in Israel's ordinary civilian courts instead. In those civilian courts, a separate new offence carries the death penalty only for killing committed with the specific intent to deny the existence of the State of Israel — a motive test that 25 Israeli international-law academics, writing under their own names, say is unlikely to be attributed to a Jewish extremist convicted of a comparable killing, even though such a person would be jurisdictionally eligible for a civilian trial. This two-track design is why the UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination, Human Rights Watch, Adalah and other rights and legal-advocacy groups — largely the same set of critics of the law, not independent constituencies — call it discriminatory.

This answer describes Israel's new death-penalty law's own jurisdictional and definitional mechanism as reported by legal scholars and rights organisations — not Bleep's own reading of the statute's primary Hebrew text, which could not be independently captured. It is a description of the rules on paper, not a report of how they have been applied in practice: no prosecution has yet reached a death sentence under this law, so no court has yet interpreted the motive requirement in a real case.

What this data can and cannot tell you

  • This page's account of Israel's death-penalty law draws on convergent rights-group, legal-scholar and journalism sources, but that convergence has real limits: several of the reporting outlets reproduce Ben-Gvir's quotes and description word for word, pointing to a shared official translation rather than independent verification, and the law's harshest critics — HRW, the UN's anti-racism committee, Adalah and 25 legal scholars — are all reading the same secondary accounts of the statute, not the primary Hebrew text itself, which the Knesset's own press release page rendered as blank on repeated capture attempts.
  • Ben-Gvir's own quotes about the gallows announcement appear across five news outlets, but several match word for word — evidence of a shared official translation, not five independent transcriptions — and none is a direct capture of his own Instagram post, which a cookie-consent barrier blocked during this research.
  • One legal challenge against Israel's new death-penalty law is confirmed here in full detail, by case number and petitioner list (HCJ 76304-03-26, filed at Israel's High Court of Justice); a further challenge is reported by a different source with an overlapping but not identical petitioner list, which this page could not confirm is genuinely separate rather than the same coalition described imprecisely — so this page does not assert a second, distinct challenge as confirmed.
  • This page's custody chart is not a death-penalty or death-row count: the 9,323 Palestinians and others in Israeli custody (August 2026) are held across every offence and detention type, including people held without charge, and none is known to have been convicted or even chargeable under Israel's new death-penalty law, which reaches only intentional-killing convictions meeting its own narrow criteria.
  • No death sentence has yet been handed down under Israel's new death-penalty law, and no execution has taken place under it — Israel's only judicial execution remains Adolf Eichmann's, in 1962 — and the High Court challenge against the law remains open and unresolved as of this page's last update.
  • The photograph accompanying the viral post under fact-check was already circulating online by at least 25 May 2026 — an earliest-confirmable date from a byline, not necessarily its first appearance — about three months before Ben-Gvir's 18 August 2026 gallows announcement. Because it predates the announcement, it cannot be a photograph of the hanging facility described in that announcement; but its precise origin, who is pictured, and what specific incident it shows could not be confirmed from any credited source.
