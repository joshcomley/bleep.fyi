How does American politics work?

America divides national power between three branches: Congress makes laws, the President carries them out, and the courts decide what the law means. Each has ways to limit the others.

1. The basic structure

State delegates drafted the Constitution in Philadelphia in 1787, after independence from Britain, to replace a weak earlier national system. The new federal government began operating in 1789.

The Constitution is America’s highest law. It sets the rules for government and protects rights. The federal government governs the whole country; each state also has its own government. The three federal branches are legislative (Congress), executive (President and departments), and judicial (courts).

The first ten amendments (additions to the Constitution), ratified in 1791, are the Bill of Rights. Familiar examples: the First Amendment protects speech and religious freedom against government interference; the Second protects the right to keep and bear arms; and “pleading the Fifth” refers to the Fifth Amendment’s protection against being forced to give testimony that could implicate you in a crime.

2. Congress: the House and the Senate

Congress is the national legislature: the House of Representatives and the Senate together. These are its two chambers. Both make federal laws, approve spending and oversee the executive branch. Congress can also declare war.

How it began: Both chambers were created by the 1787 Constitution and began work in 1789. This was a compromise: larger states wanted representation by population; smaller states wanted an equal voice. The House provides the first, the Senate the second. Senators were originally chosen by state legislatures; the 17th Amendment made them directly elected in 1913.

A bill is a proposed law. Both chambers must approve the same text, then send it to the President. The President can sign it into law or veto it (reject it). Two-thirds of each chamber can override a veto.

Impeachment means bringing formal charges, not automatically removing someone. The House charges; the Senate holds the trial. Conviction requires two-thirds of senators present and removes the official. Treaty approval also requires two-thirds of senators present.

“Having the House” means a party holds a majority of seats. It usually chooses the Speaker (the House’s leader), leads committees (smaller groups that examine bills and investigate), and largely controls which bills get votes. It can block the President’s legislation, but needs the Senate to pass laws. Senate control gives influence over legislation and confirmations. A majority does not guarantee every bill passes.

3. The President and departments

How it began: The Constitution created the presidency; George Washington became the first President in 1789. It gave the country a separate national executive, while dividing power so the President could not rule alone.

The President leads the executive branch: implements federal laws, directs foreign policy and commands the armed forces. Executive orders direct the executive branch but must stay within the Constitution and lawful presidential powers. The President cannot simply rewrite laws or spend money Congress has not authorised. The Vice President takes over if the President cannot serve and can break tied Senate votes.

Departments carry out federal policy. The President nominates (proposes) their heads and the Senate confirms (approves) them; voters do not elect them. These heads form the core of the Cabinet, the President’s senior advisers. Major departments include: State: diplomacy; Defense: military; Treasury: taxes and government finances; Justice: federal law enforcement; Health and Human Services: health and social programmes; Homeland Security: borders, domestic security and disaster response.

4. The Supreme Court

How it developed: The Constitution created the Supreme Court. In Marbury v. Madison (1803), the Court established its power to strike down unconstitutional federal laws: the principle called judicial review.

The Supreme Court is the highest court: nine justices (judges) who decide major questions of federal law and the Constitution. In cases before them, courts can strike down unconstitutional laws or government actions. The President nominates a justice when a vacancy arises; the Senate votes to confirm. The public does not elect them. They serve for life, unless they leave or are removed through impeachment. The President cannot order their rulings.

5. Elections: who is chosen, and when?

Eligible voters elect House members, senators and the President separately. Democrats and Republicans are the two main parties; a party can win the presidency while losing one or both chambers. Regular federal elections happen in November in even-numbered years. Every 4 years is a presidential election; midterms elect Congress, not a President, halfway through the presidential term. Both elect the whole House and roughly a third of the Senate.

Before November, parties select candidates through state votes called primaries or local meetings called caucuses; conventions formally nominate their ticket. Voters then choose a President–Vice President ticket. Their state results determine Electoral College votes: each state gets its House seats plus its two Senate seats; Washington, D.C. gets three. Most states award all their votes to their winner; Maine and Nebraska divide theirs. Winning needs 270 of 538 electoral votes—not necessarily the most individual votes nationwide. A President serves 4 years and can be elected at most twice.

How voting changed: Later amendments expanded voting rights: they barred denying the vote because of race (15th Amendment, 1870) or sex (19th, 1920), and protected voting from age 18 (26th, 1971).

6. State and local government

Why power is shared: The states existed before the Constitution. The settlement kept a national government alongside them; the 10th Amendment reserves powers neither assigned to the federal government nor forbidden to the states to the states or the people.

States elect governors and state legislatures on schedules that vary. Local voters choose officials such as mayors and councils. These governments handle much everyday policy, including schools, policing and roads, within federal and state law.
